<h1 class="mb-3">The Influence of Social TV Multitasking Behavior on the Effectiveness of Cross-Media Advertising</h1>
<p class="lead mb-4">How should advertising on a second screen change when television viewing becomes a social, multitasking experience?</p>

<section class="research-section"><h2>Research question and why it matters</h2><p>Television viewing increasingly extends across screens. A viewer may watch a program on one screen while messaging another person and opening related advertising on a second device. In that divided-attention environment, the most vivid advertisement is not necessarily the most effective one. This study examined how social interaction and advertising modality work together to shape attention, experience, and response.</p></section>

    </figure><p>I conducted a 2 × 2 between-subjects experiment with 240 participants in northern Taiwan. The study compared individual TV viewing with social TV viewing, and picture advertisements with video advertisements on a second screen. Participants watched approximately 40 minutes of television and used a provided device to scan QR codes and view product information.</p><p>In the social TV condition, paired participants watched the same program from separate rooms and communicated through instant messaging. The study measured brand recall, attitudes toward the advertisement and brand, purchase intention, media-use gratification, and flow. Prior social-media use and media-multitasking behavior were included as covariates.</p></section>

<section class="research-section research-contribution"><h2>My contribution</h2><p>This research originated as my master's thesis in Communication Studies at National Chiao Tung University in Taiwan. I initiated and led the project from experimental design through publication.</p><ul><li>I developed the research questions, hypotheses, and 2 × 2 experimental design.</li><li>I prepared the study materials and independently recruited 240 participants.</li><li>I conducted the in-person study sessions and managed the resulting data.</li><li>I performed the statistical analyses and interpreted the findings.</li><li>I wrote the manuscript and developed the thesis into a journal article.</li><li>I presented the research at ICA 2019 in Washington, D.C., and participated in the moderated panel's audience Q&amp;A.</li></ul><p class="small text-muted mb-0">My advisor reviewed the study design, analyses, and manuscript, and advised on publication strategy.</p></section>

<section class="research-section"><h2>Analysis</h2><p>I used descriptive statistics, reliability analysis, and correlations to examine the measures, followed by ANCOVA to test the interaction between viewing condition and advertisement modality. I then used bootstrapped path analyses to examine whether media-use gratification and flow helped explain the relationship between viewing context and advertising effectiveness.</p></section>

<section class="research-section"><h2>Key findings</h2>
<div class="research-finding"><h3>Advertisement format worked differently across viewing contexts</h3><p>Video advertisements produced better advertising effectiveness during social TV viewing, while picture advertisements performed better during individual viewing. The interaction—not advertisement modality alone—was the meaningful result.</p></div>
<div class="research-finding"><h3>Gratification helped explain advertising effectiveness</h3><p>Gratification from media use partially mediated the relationship between viewing condition and advertising effectiveness, connecting the quality of the viewing experience with participants' responses to ads.</p></div>
<div class="research-finding"><h3>Flow was a key pathway</h3><p>Flow experience fully mediated the relationship between viewing condition and advertising effectiveness, highlighting the importance of immersion when attention is distributed across screens.</p></div></section>

<section class="research-section"><h2>Design implications</h2><p>Cross-media campaigns should be designed for the viewing context rather than treating video as universally more engaging. In socially interactive settings, vivid video can help reclaim limited attention. During more focused individual viewing, a less intrusive picture can be more effective. More broadly, the study shows why product and communication decisions should account for users' social context, cognitive load, and movement between devices.</p></section>

<section class="research-section"><h2>From thesis to publication</h2><p>I presented this work at the 2019 International Communication Association Annual Conference in Washington, D.C., as part of a moderated conference panel followed by an audience Q&amp;A. I subsequently developed the thesis into a manuscript that was accepted by the <em>International Journal of Communication</em> and published in 2021.</p></section>

<section class="research-section"><h2>What I carried forward</h2><p>This project taught me how to carry a research question through every stage of an empirical study: translating theory into an experimental design, coordinating a large in-person sample, analyzing interaction and mediation effects, and communicating the findings to both conference and journal audiences. It also established the question that continues to shape my work: how can we rigorously measure what people perceive while interacting with technology?</p></section>
